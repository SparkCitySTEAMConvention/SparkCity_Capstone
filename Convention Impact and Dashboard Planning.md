Convention Impact and Dashboard Planning

Summary:  
**Main Topics Discussed**

The group reviewed their project presentations and how to frame their analyses around the convention’s impact. They agreed that the work should focus on infrastructure and capacity rather than trying to build a predictive model. The discussion centered on using the data they already have—especially occupancy and energy readings—and turning that into a clear story for the audience.

**Occupancy and energy analysis**

• One person planned to use average occupancy by month and present the top 3 months after reviewing the trend, rather than showing all 12 months individually.

• They discussed plotting the monthly averages in a graph so the audience can see which months are most and least favorable.

• For energy readings, the consensus was that they do not fluctuate much across the year, so a monthly average plot would likely show little variation but could still be included for completeness.

• They noted that occupancy appears to be broadly similar across building tiers, so breaking it down by small, medium, or large building type may not add much value.

**Infrastructure** **impact**

**The most useful framing was identified as: How will the convention affect the city’s infrastructure if 15,000 more people come in?**

• They talked about using any available infrastructure-related data, even though they only currently have occupancy and energy data.

• Potential impacts mentioned included parking, trains, traffic, and general city congestion.

• The conversation referenced how the World Cup caused confusion and frustration because visitors underestimated distances between places like New Jersey, New York, Delaware, and Philadelphia, and many did not realize how much travel time and transit planning would be required.

• This was seen as a strong example to justify the importance of infrastructure planning for the convention.

**Data limitations and unused variables**

• They questioned whether the residential type or city zone data would be useful.

• The group concluded that those variables mostly describe what kind of area a location is (residential, commercial, industrial, maybe parks) and likely do not directly support the current analysis.

• They also believed the building count / zoning information would not help much unless it included something like parking garages.

• No one felt comfortable inventing data, and they decided to stick with the real information available.

**Convention planning and suitability scoring**

A second part of the discussion focused on the convention planner webpage and its design:

• One person needs to explain why a particular day or week was chosen.

• They liked the idea of adding a slider/toggle bar to adjust a weighted scale or sensitivity setting, so users can see how changing the weights affects the result.

• The term “sensitivity assessment” came up as a possible name for the tool.

• Someone needs the raw data and the suitability scores for all months so they can integrate them into a dropdown and compare results across the year.

• The plan is to support all 12 months, not just the two months currently being compared.

• The landing page may default to April, with a dropdown that lets users switch to other months; right now it only shows February and April.

• They discussed possibly replacing or reorganizing the existing month comparison section, since the dropdown and slider might make that unnecessary.

• The page should still include the overall suitability score, factors/breakdown, key takeaways, and the proposed recommendation.

**Design and presentation choices**

• They agreed that the app should show the weighted scale breakdown and explain the logic behind the initial weights.

• The group reaffirmed their weighting priorities:

• Capacity should be the top factor, because the city must be able to house everyone.

• Fiscal impact should be next, to determine whether the convention will create negative financial effects.
